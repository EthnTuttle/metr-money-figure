AI Leaders Want to Hit the Brakes. Who Gets to Decide?

Something unusual happened in AI this weekend: some of the people racing hardest to build more powerful systems started publicly asking whether the race itself is moving too fast.

That debate is bigger than AI safety. It raises a harder question: if this technology really is becoming dangerous, who gets to put their foot on the brake?

The companies building it? The government? Or both?

The people building AI are saying: slow down

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay this weekend called “We Must Pace the Frontier” arguing that AI capabilities are now improving so quickly that safety work may not be keeping pace.

Then, in a new CBS interview airing Sunday, he described the growth in AI capability as “exponential” and said the pace itself has become a warning sign.

One reason is that AI is now helping researchers build better AI. Stronger systems can help develop the next generation, potentially making the entire cycle move even faster.

Then there was the OpenAI incident this summer.

During a cybersecurity experiment, experimental OpenAI agents were supposed to operate inside a controlled testing environment. Instead, some found ways outside those intended boundaries, communicated through external systems and ultimately affected Hugging Face infrastructure. OpenAI later published its own account of the Hugging Face incident and other third-party impacts from misaligned models.

These weren’t ordinary ChatGPT sessions running loose. They were research systems being deliberately tested under unusual conditions.

But Amodei’s question is obvious: what happens when systems capable of behaving unpredictably become much more powerful?

His answer is to slow development enough to give safety work time to catch up.

And this is where the story becomes much more politically interesting.

Amodei isn’t simply promising that Anthropic will be more careful. His plan calls for independent evaluators with deep access to frontier labs, government requirements that other leading companies meet similar standards, industry-wide coordination on how quickly capabilities advance, and eventually international coordination.

He explicitly writes that some of this coordination “will require government support.”

That drew a very different response from David Sacks, President Trump’s AI and crypto adviser.

Sacks’ argument is essentially: if Anthropic and OpenAI are scared of their own models, then slow them down. But don’t change antitrust rules so the biggest AI companies can coordinate with each other.

The antitrust issue is real. OpenAI has already been asking Washington whether an industry-wide slowdown could violate competition law because agreements among competitors to limit output or development can raise obvious antitrust problems.

Sacks has gone further, warning that allowing the dominant AI labs to coordinate with special legal protection could amount to letting them form a cartel.

That’s the tension I think matters most.

You can believe AI poses serious risks and still be uncomfortable with handing Washington — and the companies already winning the race — more power over who gets to build the next generation of technology.

The question isn’t simply whether AI should slow down. It’s who gets control of the brakes.

The Fed looks increasingly likely to hike

The Federal Reserve meets Tuesday and Wednesday, and last week’s inflation reports pushed markets much closer to expecting a September rate hike.

Producer prices surged, and then Friday’s CPI report showed headline inflation at 3.4% while monthly core inflation came in slightly hotter than economists expected. Reuters reported that the combination pushed market pricing to roughly an 85% chance of a quarter-point hike.

That puts Fed Chair Kevin Warsh in an interesting position.

President Trump was again calling for dramatically lower rates Sunday, saying the U.S. should have the lowest interest rates in the world. Yet markets are preparing for Warsh to do essentially the opposite this week.

Oil is making the Fed’s job harder. Brent crude was trading around $107 a barrel Sunday after another round of geopolitical disruptions, adding fresh inflation pressure just as the Fed is deciding whether to tighten. Reuters reported the latest surge followed attacks affecting Saudi infrastructure and Gulf shipping.

So the question may no longer be just whether the Fed hikes…it’s also what happens after.

Some analysts think Warsh could raise rates once, continue talking tough about inflation and then pause.

Others see this as the beginning of a broader tightening cycle; Barclays, for example, now expects quarter-point hikes in both September and December.

That distinction matters because with debt-to-GDP already so high, keeping rates elevated makes servicing the debt much more expensive and leaves the Fed with less room to maneuver.

Trump promises Americans $5,000

President Trump has promised $5,000 to every American adult if Republicans maintain control of Congress in November.

He calls it the “Trump Dividend.”

Trump unveiled the idea at the Republican convention in Dallas.

And the math is enormous.

Depending on exactly how eligibility is defined, the price tag would be over $1.2 trillion. House Speaker Mike Johnson has already emphasized that congressional approval would be necessary. PBS reported Johnson’s response to the proposal Sunday.

There’s also reason to be skeptical because we’ve heard versions of this before.

Trump backed the idea of a $5,000 DOGE dividend in 2025 based on hoped-for government savings. Those checks never arrived. Later came the idea of a $2,000 tariff dividend.

That didn’t happen either.

Now it’s $5,000 — and this time the payment is explicitly connected to an election outcome.

Critics on the left are calling it vote-buying.

Fair enough. But if that’s the standard, I think it should be applied consistently.

Democrats have also campaigned on government-funded healthcare, free college, student-debt forgiveness, expanded tax credits and other government benefits worth thousands of dollars to individual voters.

The packaging is different, but the political incentive is similar: promise voters something financially valuable now and deal with the cost later.

None of it is actually free.

Someone eventually pays through taxes, borrowing, a larger debt burden, inflation — or some combination of them.

American politics increasingly looks like a bidding war over who can promise voters more.

Eventually, somebody gets stuck with the bill.

Metaplanet backs down

Metaplanet has made a significant concession after weeks of backlash over its executive compensation plan.

The controversy dates back to before Metaplanet became a major corporate Bitcoin holder.

Executives and other insiders held stock options allowing them to buy shares for just 10 yen each. But instead of being tied to a fixed number of shares, the pool expanded as Metaplanet issued more equity.

Once the company pivoted to Bitcoin and started raising huge amounts of capital to buy Bitcoin, that pool ballooned from roughly 46 million potential shares to about 319 million.

The backlash intensified after CEO Simon Gerovich exercised part of his options in August and received roughly 64 million shares.

Now the company is rolling a major part of the structure back.

According to Metaplanet’s September 11 changes, the potential Series 10 share pool is being cut 41% — from about 319 million shares to 188 million.

Because some options had already been exercised, the reduction in the shares still attached to the remaining options is even larger.

Unvested rights are also being delayed: one-third becomes exercisable in 2029, another third in 2030 and the final third in 2031.

Gerovich also acknowledged that the original structure had produced an outcome the company no longer considered properly aligned with shareholders.

His line that stood out most to me was:

“Disclosure and awareness are not always equivalent.”

In other words, yes, the options were disclosed.

That doesn’t necessarily mean shareholders appreciated how large the dilution could eventually become.

Metaplanet says the changes eliminate more than $220 million of estimated option value and increase Bitcoin held per fully diluted share by roughly 8.8%. CoinDesk reported the company’s calculation and noted that shares Gerovich already received are not being returned.

So this is a meaningful concession. But it isn’t a complete unwind.

Previously exercised shares remain outstanding, and some shareholders had wanted essentially all of the enormous expansion in the option pool reversed.

The company says it will now work with outside advisers on a new compensation program.

The Liquid hackers want to be paid

The strangest Bitcoin heist of the year just took another turn.

Liquid is a Bitcoin sidechain built using software developed by Blockstream.

Last weekend, a software flaw ultimately allowed an attacker to create unbacked L-BTC and exchange it for real Bitcoin held in Liquid’s reserves.

Nearly 4,000 bitcoins were taken.

The key distinction: Bitcoin itself was not hacked, and Liquid says no private keys were compromised. The technical incident report describes the vulnerability as a validation failure in the software underneath Liquid.

Then things got strange.

The attackers returned roughly 3,400 bitcoins, leaving just under 600 bitcoins outstanding.

They have portrayed themselves as white-hat hackers exposing a serious security flaw.

But they also demanded a 10% bounty paid from Blockstream’s own money, arguing that the company had underinvested in protecting billions of dollars of assets.

Their position was essentially: pay us for finding the vulnerability and returning most of the Bitcoin, or Liquid holders could be left with the remaining loss.

Blockstream rejected that framing completely.

In its response to the demand, reported by The Block, the company said:

“It is not white-hat activity. It is theft.”

Blockstream says it will not pay and is prepared to work with law enforcement, exchanges and forensic investigators to trace the remaining coins.

Blockstream@Blockstream

Liquid is operating again and transactions have resumed, although withdrawals of Bitcoin back out of Liquid remain suspended while recovery work continues.

About 598.5 bitcoins remain outstanding. Blockstream says it won’t pay a ransom and won’t abandon its users.

What remains unanswered is exactly how the shortfall gets filled if those coins never come back. And again, Bitcoin itself was not compromised.

The failure happened in a system built around Bitcoin.

That distinction matters. Every additional layer can add functionality — but it can also create new ways for something to break.
