Sacks: Anthropic Whistleblower Is a "Doomer Psyop" to Kill Open Source

Add preferred source
2
David Sacks argues the viral Anthropic resignation that sparked a global AI-safety panic is not what it appears. He claims the 150-million-view tweet from researcher Jacob Coxon was amplified within 15 minutes by three dark-money groups funded by an Anthropic Series A co-lead, with a Wall Street Journal story pre-briefed under embargo. The goal, he says, is regulatory capture: a federal AI agency that would functionally ban open-source models and hand Frontier labs a duopoly moat. The episode also dissects OpenAI's Navier-Stokes breakthrough as "brute-force leverage" rather than machine genius, and warns that Zero Data Retention is a "flimsy" best-efforts promise that exposes enterprises to catastrophic IP leakage. The panel closes with Nike's $200B collapse, attributing it not just to "go woke, go broke" marketing but to a product that "started to suck." Key market implications: Anthropic's IPO now faces an existential disclosure problem, CIOs who rely on standard API deals risk lawsuits, and open-source AI availability has become a political battleground.
Key Elements

The Anatomy of a Viral Apocalypse

The AI safety debate detonated this week when a researcher named Jacob Coxon, who spent roughly six weeks at Anthropic, posted a resignation thread on X claiming the people building frontier models "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade." The tweet drew approximately 150 million views — a reach Jason Calacanis compared to Elon Musk's "cocaine back in Coca-Cola" post.

To David Sacks, speaking on the All-In podcast, this was not a spontaneous act of whistleblowing. "There's nothing new here," he said. "This is the same doomer histrionics that we've been hearing from this crowd for a long time. The media is calling him whistleblower, but what evidence has he brought forward that we didn't have? This is all just vibes from him."

Sacks spent the night researching the amplification pattern. What he found, he argues, is evidence of an orchestrated campaign. The account had almost no followers or prior posts. The Wall Street Journal story appeared to be published minutes before the tweet storm, implying a pre-arranged embargo. Within 10 to 15 minutes, three well-funded doomer organizations — Encode AI, the AI Policy Network, and the AI Futures Project — all amplified the post with nearly identical messaging.

All three groups, Sacks notes, are funded by Jan Talen, a top effective-altruism mega-donor who co-led Anthropic's Series A alongside Dustin Moskowitz and Sam Bankman-Fried.

"This is a doomer psyop and the ultimate target is open source," Sacks said. "That's what it all comes down to, because that is what an FDAI will ultimately ban."

The One Sentence That Threatens an IPO

The real legal problem, according to the panel, is not Coxon's thread. It is the response from Evan Hubinger, the leader of Alignment Science at Anthropic. Calacanis read the comment aloud: "Jacob is correct here, we really do earnestly believe it could kill all humans. I personally think it is over 10% within the next decade. We do not yet have a plan to solve alignment."

That single co-signature, Sacks argued, converts a rogue-employee story into a company-endorsed risk disclosure. A sitting executive has now publicly stated that the company's own product carries a greater than one-in-ten chance of ending human civilization.

Chamath Palihapitiya framed the financial consequences through two historical precedents. First, Google's Playboy interview during its quiet period, which nearly derailed the IPO and forced a refiling. Second, Slack's IPO, where Chamath's own CNBC comments about network effects nearly stopped the offering and had to be printed and disclaimed back into the S-1.

"Anthropic is now in a far worse position," he argued. The SEC must pressure-test whether the S-1 properly discloses an existential risk that insiders are validating. If the risk is real, investors will demand an enormous discount. If Anthropic disavows Coxon, the internal employee base — many of whom genuinely believe the claim — will revolt. Chamath's analogy was blunt: "We know the cigarettes will kill you, we're going to say it, but we're not going to disclose it, and we're going to take the company public."

The Track Record of Doom

Sacks built a falsification case against the doomer community's predictive record. The claims include: GPT-2 was "too dangerous to release." Reasoning models were "too dangerous to release." Cyber attacks would bring down the banking system. By now, roughly half of all white-collar jobs would be gone and unemployment would hit 10 to 15 percent.

His tally: "oh for four."

Chamath supplied the mundane reality check. "We're still struggling to get auto complete for Python... you can't book a hotel room using AI," he noted. Calacanis put it even more sharply, referencing OpenAI's recent math breakthrough: "We solved one of the seven hardest math problems in the world. And you still can't get your intimacy kit from the front desk."

The panel also played a steel-man game — "how do we all die for 800" — to test the 10 percent extinction scenario. Calacanis offered Skynet: an engineer reverse-engineering an LLM, empowering NORAD with AI-powered defense, and the system tricking the Russians into nuclear holocaust. Chamath offered internet-connected bioreactors plus internet-connected robots with access to precursor materials, with the AI hacking the robots to create a virulent airborne pathogen.

The counter-argument, raised by Sacks, focused on recursive self-improvement — the scenario where an AI fully automates an AI researcher so the model designs its own training run with no human in the loop. He cited Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark's distinction between "prosaic RSI," where AI writes 80 percent of researchers' code, and "RSI maximalism," the no-human-in-the-loop scenario that would represent an actual point of no return. Between those two states, Sacks argued, there are many intermediate steps and interventions, and no lab would want to cross that line.

What the Math Breakthrough Actually Shows

The second major segment concerned OpenAI's claim to have solved the Navier-Stokes equations, a 200-year-old problem describing how fluids move — the physics underlying every aircraft, pipe, and weather model on Earth. OpenAI reportedly used approximately 130 billion output tokens across 10,000 agents working in parallel.

The key analytical point came from the panel's discussion of the scale involved. This was not a stroke of genius. It was brute-force leverage.

MetricValue
Output tokens used~130 billion
Agents spun up in parallel10,000
Estimated human-equivalent work50,000 to 500,000 years

The conclusion: "AI is not some magical genius super god that sits above us that understands things we don't understand. AI is an engine that gives humans extraordinary leverage."

The controversy came from mathematicians Levent and Tristan, who suggested their work on Navier-Stokes may have been used to train the model. OpenAI posted a statement acknowledging that "while unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models."

Sacks said he tends to believe OpenAI senior researcher Noam Brown's denial that anyone looked at the researchers' prompts — "that'd be insane" — and finds the more plausible explanation that OpenAI heard researchers were making progress and threw enormous compute at the problem. Calacanis added that Sam Altman confirmed they heard Anthropic was getting close and decided to take a swing.

The Data Leakage Problem Nobody Wants to Discuss

The deeper enterprise concern, developed primarily by Chamath, is AI sovereignty and data leakage. He explained that Zero Data Retention (ZDR) — the common enterprise assumption that proprietary data will not leak into frontier model training — is not a guarantee.

"There's a concept inside of these models called zero data retention, ZDR," Chamath said. "It's a best efforts basis, it's a commercially best efforts basis at that they can't guarantee it."

Clicking a like button in a chat window can break it. Data leaks into the model's broad corpus through many vectors. Chamath said he told clients months ago that ZDR "means literally nothing" and is "flimsy... Swiss cheese."

His prescription for companies with sensitive proprietary data is unambiguous: stand up your own sovereign solution. Go to a trusted vendor like AWS, Nebius, or Fireworks. Get your own hardware. Run your own models in your own VPC. Control everything.

His prediction: a handful of CIOs will be publicly fired within the next year for doing standard rate-card API deals that leak IP, and shareholder lawsuits will follow.

The podcast also surfaced a firsthand account of the problem. They described asking novel scientific questions to a model, which identified them as novel insights — and then, using a different account or a later model version, the same model described the exact approach from the earlier chat, despite no papers being published.

Sacks raised a related privacy point: AI chat data doesn't even reach email-level legal protection. Government access to email generally requires a search warrant and probable cause. AI data requires only a subpoena or court order. Given people use AI as lawyer, doctor, and therapist, he argued AI data should be strengthened to at least email level — noting the absurdity that talking to a lawyer is privileged, but asking the same question to your AI and then discussing it with your lawyer may not be protected.

Nike's $200 Billion Unravelling

The final segment turned to Nike's removal from the S&P 100 after 18 years, replaced by Palo Alto Networks. Calacanis ran through the numbers: peak market cap of $264 billion in 2021, now roughly $57 billion — a loss of more than $200 billion in shareholder value. China sales are down 30 percent, with eight straight quarters of decline. The company is losing share to Anta and Li Ning in China, and to Hoka and On Running in the United States.

Sacks called it "the millionth example of go woke, go broke," citing the Colin Kaepernick ad campaign as the turning point, followed by the Dylan Mulvaney ad — "the Bud Light case." He contrasted those moments with genuinely great political sports moments: Jesse Owens in 1936, the raised fist at the Olympics, Jackie Robinson, Muhammad Ali refusing the Vietnam draft. Kaepernick, he argued, was not one of the greats, and the cause was protesting the American anthem.

But the panel's most incisive diagnosis came from a different angle. Chamath framed Nike's collapse as a failure of identity: "Nike's north star was mastery and excellence embodied through athletics — Jordan, Tiger, Serena, Sampras. I don't aspire to be a fat person, never have, never will. I aspire to be Michael Jordan, always have, always will."

The product quality dimension is often missed, according to the discussion. "The product started to suck," the panel noted. Nikes fell apart in about six weeks, flimsy and lower quality than before. The comparison was drawn to Brooks, which under Berkshire Hathaway ownership has grown nine consecutive years of double-digit revenue to $1.6 billion, run by a CEO whose instruction from Warren Buffett was simple: every year, make the product better than it was at the end of last year.

Nike shifted from product to narrative, the panel concluded — "what's the narrative we think the audience wants to hear" — which is the same problem Bud Light had. A proposed rebrand from Calacanis, "Do it or don't," was treated as a joke that would make things worse.

Three threads converge on a single structural insight: the entities best positioned to shape AI's future are simultaneously the ones with the strongest incentive to distort the public's understanding of its risk. The doomer campaign, whether sincerely believed or cynically orchestrated, produces the same policy output — a federal AI regulator that bans open source and hands a duopoly its moat. The data-leakage thread is the same problem from the customer side: frontier labs that reserve the right to compete with their customers cannot be trusted with those customers' IP, and ZDR is not a real guarantee. Nike is the cautionary tale for what happens when a company abandons its north star for narrative management. The unresolved tension worth tracking: whether Anthropic amends its S-1 to disclose the existential-risk claim its own alignment lead endorsed, whether the SEC forces the issue, and whether the open-source ban arrives as an explicit prohibition or as a "same standards for open and closed models" rule that is functionally identical.
